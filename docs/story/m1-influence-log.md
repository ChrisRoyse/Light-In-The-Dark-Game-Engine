# M1 Influence Log

Required by doctrine #1848 rule 4: a lightweight influence log per campaign chapter --
which patterns informed the content -- for legal traceability, NOT attribution. Also
records the research behind the engineered emotion (#1848 rule 5) and the franchise-
collision searches behind the originality claim (#1848 rule 1). ASCII-only.

Sourcing discipline (rule 4): only PUBLIC-DOMAIN and CC0 sources were studied as
STRUCTURAL LORE inspiration. No CC BY (attribution burden) and never CC BY-SA (viral)
lore sources. The PD tragedies below were studied for STRUCTURE only (irreversible
choice, doomed loyalty, oath-cost, tragedy-from-error); no names, creatures, deities,
places, or plots from them are reproduced. The psychology and RTS-craft references are
METHOD references (how to engineer attachment and pace a campaign), not lore sources;
none of their expression is reproduced -- they inform technique, not text.

Target: 100% original expression informed by commons-level patterns. No borrowing ratio.

## Part 1 -- PD structural-lore inspirations (studied for STRUCTURE only)

All five are public domain. Each entry: what PATTERN we took, and where it appears.

1. Aristotle, "Poetics" (classical; PUBLIC DOMAIN).
   URLs studied: classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/poetics.2.2.html;
   perseus.tufts.edu Poetics 1452a/1453a/1455a; internetshakespeare.uvic.ca Poetics.
   Patterns taken:
   - Tragedy is most powerful when events are unexpected yet follow as cause-and-effect
     ("propter hoc," not "post hoc") and the outcome feels inevitable. -> Miren's death is
     foreshadowed and made un-preventable (Mission 3), not a random shock.
   - Peripeteia (reversal) coincident with anagnorisis (recognition) produces pity and
     fear. -> Mission 4's fog-clearing recognition that Yvenne struck the refugee column.
   - The tragic figure is "a man like ourselves" who falls through error (hamartia), not
     villainy. -> the whole conflict is error-under-fog between sympathetic peoples; Miren
     falls through the excess of her virtue.
   Used in: world frame (inevitability), heroes (Miren, Yvenne), campaign M3/M4.

2. The Kalevala, incl. the Kullervo cycle (runes 31-36), compiled by Elias Lonnrot;
   Kirby/Crawford translations (PUBLIC DOMAIN; Project Gutenberg #7000, Wikisource).
   Patterns taken:
   - A doom that cannot be escaped and a grief that cannot be undone; a tragedy built
     from mistreatment and irreversible acts (the broken heirloom, the irreversible
     recognition, the refusal to be turned aside). We took the SHAPE of inevitability and
     of an act that can never be taken back -- NOT Kullervo, his family, his deeds, or any
     name or plot point.
   Used in: world frame (the Unturning as felt doom), heroes (irreversibility stances),
   campaign (the un-take-back-able event).

3. Norse mythology: the Poetic Edda and Prose Edda (Snorri Sturluson / Saemund);
   Thorpe & Blackwell translation (PUBLIC DOMAIN; Project Gutenberg #14726). Framing
   reference: norse-mythology.org concept article on Wyrd/Urd (consulted for orientation,
   not copied).
   Patterns taken:
   - Fate (wyrd) is inexorable; even the powerful cannot change it -- and honor lies not in
     escaping doom but in how one MEETS a loss known to be certain (fight to the last;
     amor fati). -> the Emberhold ethos ("hold"), the campaign title "Held to the Last,"
     Miren holding the ward knowingly, the finale's carry-the-loss-forward resolution.
   No god, giant, place, or event name is reused.
   Used in: world frame, factions (Emberhold), campaign M3/M5.

4. Volsunga saga (Old Norse legendary saga; PUBLIC DOMAIN). Studied via scholarly
   summary (Garault, "I am the master of my fate," Univ. of Iceland thesis, consulted for
   analysis of fate/honor/oath -- the thesis is copyrighted scholarship used as ANALYSIS,
   not a lore source).
   Patterns taken:
   - The geometry of doomed loyalty: an oath, once sworn, compels tragic action even
     against the swearer's wish; honor and fate bind the hand. -> wardsmithing's oath-logic
     (a sworn ward cannot be lowered), Caul's oath to keep secrets, the factions' cut oaths
     that cannot be unsworn.
   Used in: world frame (oath-cost), factions, heroes (Caul, Miren), campaign M2/M5.

5. Greek myth: the Moirai (the Fates), esp. Atropos, "the unturning one" (a- "not" +
   tropos "turning") (classical; PUBLIC DOMAIN).
   Pattern taken:
   - An inexorable fate that is never turned aside -- the literal meaning of the name
     Atropos. We took this STRUCTURAL idea and the plain-English gloss to coin the world's
     metaphysical law, "the Unturning." NO Greek name (Atropos, Clotho, Lachesis, the
     Moirai), deity, thread/spindle/shears iconography, or plot is reproduced; only the
     abstract idea and its literal English translation are used. This is the documented
     lineage behind "the Unturning" (operator keep decision, 2026-07-14; see
     docs/story/m1-anti-recognizability.md).
   Used in: world frame (the Unturning as the law that nothing reverses).

## Part 2 -- Emotion/craft METHOD references (technique, not lore; not reproduced)

These are copyrighted research/craft works consulted for HOW to engineer attachment and
loss, per #1848 rule 5. They are not lore sources and no expression from them is shipped.

Parasocial attachment and grief at character loss:
- Cohen, J. (2004). Parasocial Break-Up from Favorite Television Characters. J. Social
  and Personal Relationships 21(2), 187-202. -> anticipated loss of a favored character
  produces real breakup-like distress; attachment intensity drives it.
- Lather & Moyer-Guse (2011), temporary parasocial breakup; DeGroot & Leith (2015),
  parasocial grief for a dead TV character. -> character death yields genuine grief.
- Rain & Mar (2021) (via systematic reviews consulted). -> anxiously attached audiences
  bond more strongly to sociotropic/protective ("caring, protective") characters. ->
  shaped Sister Miren (the protective caretaker) as the highest-attachment figure.
Applied to: heroes (attachment hooks), the decision to make the protective healer the loss.

Identifiable-victim / singularity effect and affect:
- Jenni & Loewenstein (1997); Small, Loewenstein & Slovic (2007); Kogut & Ritov
  (identifiable victim / singularity). Slovic (affect heuristic; psychic numbing). Note:
  some effects are contested in recent replications (Maier et al.; Hart, Lane & Chinn;
  the "scope-insensitivity" reframing) -- we rely on the robust core: a single, named,
  vivid individual evokes stronger affect than a statistic or a crowd.
Applied to: one named loss (Miren), not a faceless body count, to maximize felt grief.

Loss aversion / prospect theory:
- Kahneman & Tversky (1979), prospect theory; risk-seeking for losses; the certainty
  effect (via Loewenstein/Small "Explaining the Identifiable Victim Effect"). -> a
  certain, foreseeable loss the player is powerless to trade away hits harder than a
  probabilistic one.
Applied to: engineering M3 so the loss is certain and un-preventable, not a dice roll.

Character want vs need / the Lie / the wound (craft):
- K.M. Weiland, "Creating Character Arcs" ("the Lie the character believes"); September C.
  Fawkes (the ghost/wound -> flawed worldview -> want); Final Draft and No Film School
  want-vs-need explainers; alanwatt.com and plotiar.com on want/need and scene-level arc.
Applied to: every hero profile (want, need, the specific Lie, the specific wound).

Permadeath and meaningful loss in games (directly relevant to our no-undo doctrine):
- West, Cohen, Banks & Goodboy (2022), "It's all fun and games until somebody dies:
  Permadeath appreciation as a function of grief and mortality salience," J. Gaming &
  Virtual Worlds 14(2). -> permadeath recollections increase appreciation, mediated by
  grief; the effect is stronger for players with stronger parasocial attachment to the
  character. This is the empirical backbone of the doctrine's "emotional edge."
- Carter & Allison (2017), "Fear, loss and meaningful play: Permadeath in DayZ"; Allison,
  Carter & Gibbs (2015), "Good Frustrations." -> permanent loss is positively valued when
  it is MEANINGFUL (Salen & Zimmerman's meaningful play), which requires legibility and
  consequence -- so the loss must be authored and understood, not arbitrary.
- Keogh (2013), "When game over means game over"; Lewis, Weber & Bowman (2008), character
  attachment metric. -> permadeath shifts play from experimentation to vulnerability;
  attachment is measurable and buildable.
Applied to: making Miren's death canonical, legible, and un-revivable; framing the M3
failstate as loss-not-game-over so grief -> appreciation rather than frustration.

RTS-campaign craft (structure, not content):
- "StarCraft: Legacy of the Real-Time Strategy" (OAPEN open-access book). -> the
  "decoding paradigm," hero-survival objectives, and mission-to-mission variety; heroes
  as narrative anchors.
- "That Girl in Blue" Warcraft III retrospective (parts 21, 29). -> archetype set-up then
  subversion; in-engine cutscenes and short diegetic voice lines over exposition dumps;
  the craft of introducing a unit/ability diegetically; and the concrete device of leaving
  a departing hero's items behind. -> informed our cinematic-beat approach and the
  perspective shift.
- Legacy of the Void "deotherization" analysis (SFU Loading journal). -> player/NPC
  network attachment; a hero sacrifice (Zeratul) that is memorialized and haunts the rest
  of the campaign. -> informed carrying Miren's absence forward (memorial, named gate)
  rather than dropping it.
Applied to: the five-mission shape, the M4 perspective shift, hero persistence in
save/load, and the memorial finale.
